SECTION 1: Foundations — Data, Inequity, and the Social Construction of Public Health (Weeks 1-6)

Week 1 | Jan 11-15 | Why Data Is Never Neutral

Theme: The Social Construction of Public Health Evidence

Every dataset reflects choices about what to count, who counts, and who decides. This week we examine how framing shapes public health evidence and how administrative law and agency power turn ‘data’ into ‘crisis.’ We introduce R Markdown for reproducible reporting and explore publicly available health data resources. We also examine the Google Flu Trends episode as a cautionary tale about how big data can mislead as well as illuminate. Note: Monday January 18 is Martin Luther King Jr. Day — no classes. Week 2 begins Tuesday January 19.

Required Readings

Week 2 | Jan 19-23 | Redlining, Race, and the Geography of Illness

Theme: Historical Policy Echoes: How the Past Becomes the Present

The 1930s Home Owners’ Loan Corporation did not just deny mortgages — it engineered the geography of life expectancy. This week we trace the legal history of state-sponsored residential segregation and its direct, measurable effects on contemporary health outcomes. We introduce the Opportunity Atlas, begin working with HOLC shapefiles and spatial joins, and introduce HMDA mortgage data as a tool for documenting present-day lending discrimination. This week’s lab is the technical foundation for Assignment 1, due next Friday.

Week 4 | Feb 2-6 | Social Cohesion and Health

Theme: Collective Efficacy, Trust, and Community Health

Robert Sampson’s landmark Chicago research established that collective efficacy — a neighborhood’s shared willingness to intervene for the common good — is one of the strongest predictors of health and safety outcomes, independent of poverty. This week we map social vulnerability alongside CDC PLACES health outcome data, focusing on variables that capture social isolation and lack of emotional support — dimensions of health that are often invisible in administrative data. We build bivariate maps that visually communicate the relationship between neighborhood social conditions and population health.

Week 5 | Feb 9-13 | Who Does the Algorithm Think You Are?

Theme: Algorithmic Accountability, Bias, and Political Geography

Predictive risk models now influence who gets flagged for child welfare investigation, who is triaged first in an emergency department, and who qualifies for housing assistance. Crucially, these algorithms are built on data that is itself shaped by political decisions — including redistricting, which determines who votes in which district and thus which communities get political representation and public investment. This week we examine algorithmic bias in public health AND explore how redistricting data can reveal political geographies of health inequity.

Week 6 | Feb 16-20 | The Stories Clinical Data Won’t Tell You

Structured data — ICD codes, admission diagnoses — systematically misses the context of injury. Industrial zoning is not random. Historically, hazardous facilities were sited near communities with the least political power.
